Domain: Healthcare

# PharmEasy Healthcare Decision Intelligence

A capstone built on synthetic records (800 patients, generated by `generate_data.py`). Nothing here is real PharmEasy data and nothing here is clinical advice. All money is in INR.

## How to run

From a clean clone, with Python 3.10 or newer:

```
pip install -r requirements.txt
python generate_data.py
python part1.py
python part2.py
python part3.py
python part4.py
```

`generate_data.py` must print `800 10 170 e26b46e82f1a1af44dcabc133050802e`. Each part script rewrites its files in `outputs/`. `common.py` holds shared helpers (data loading, split, logistic regression, metrics, KNN) and runs nothing when imported. Part 4 reuses the Part 2 split, model and KNN cross-validation by importing it.

## Part 1 findings

**R1.1 Planning claim.** H0 (average care-plan cost is INR 8,000) is rejected: `z_stat` = 2.8930, beyond the critical value of 1.96. A Type I error here would mean concluding that the average cost differs from INR 8,000 and changing the budget, when the true average is in fact INR 8,000. The gap `mean_value` - 8.0 is 0.9382 column units, about INR 938 per patient, roughly 12 percent above the plan. That is small next to the spread of individual costs (`sd_value` = 9.1723), but because the budget multiplies it by every patient on the plan, it is large enough to matter in practice and the planning figure should be revisited. The 95% interval for the mean is 8.3026 to 9.5738.

**R1.2 Sample size.** `n_required` = 1293 records for a margin of error of INR 500, against the 800 available. With 800 rows the margin is about 1.96 x 0.3243 = INR 636, so the team would need roughly 493 more records to reach INR 500.

**R1.3 Gradient descent.** `gd_slope_gap` is 0.0000 (below 0.001). Squared-error cost for a straight line has a single minimum, and 500 small steps on the standardised input reach it (the cost is 40.3698 at both 100 and 500 updates), so gradient descent lands on the closed-form line.

**R1.4 Funnel check.** `resid_sd_ratio_raw` = 3.7525 and `resid_sd_ratio_log` = 1.0396. The raw model clearly shows a funnel: residual spread in the upper half of fitted values is almost four times that of the lower half. The log-log model removes it, with the two halves almost equally spread.

**R1.5 Multiple regression.** Predictors with a coefficient above 0.05 in absolute value are ln(X4) (1.1576), X2 missed visits (0.0940), X3 copay ratio (0.1067) and X6 chronic condition flag (0.1518). This is a mechanical rule on coefficients in different units, not a ranking of importance. `mr_adj_r2` = 0.7403 against `loglog_adj_r2` = 0.6561, so the extra predictors earn their place: adjusted R-squared rises by about 0.08 even after the penalty for the added terms.

## Part 2 findings

**R2.1 Learning rates.** After 200 updates the losses from lowest to highest are 0.3703 (eta 1.0), 0.3776 (eta 0.1) and 0.5091 (eta 0.01). A learning rate that is too small has simply not travelled far enough in 200 steps, while the large rate still converged here without overshooting.

**R2.2 Weights.** The three largest absolute weights belong to X3 copay burden (0.9013), X1 months registered (-0.5554) and X2 missed visits (0.5505). The largest odds ratio is 2.4629 for X3: each one standard deviation rise in the share of the bill a patient pays out of pocket multiplies the odds of a no-show by about 2.5, other predictors held fixed.

**R2.3 Accuracy and thresholds.** `blind_accuracy` is 0.7875, while the model at tau = 0.5 scores 0.8042, only about 1.7 points better than predicting "attends" for everyone, so accuracy alone says little when one record in five is a no-show. Lowering tau from 0.5 to 0.3 raised recall from 0.4706 to 0.7451 and lowered precision from 0.5455 to 0.4634 (F1 rose from 0.5053 to 0.5714). Accuracy fell to 0.7625, yet the model catches far more of the no-shows.

**R2.4 Logistic versus KNN.** At tau = 0.3 the logistic model has `f1` 0.5714 and `recall` 0.7451. KNN with the cross-validated K = 5 has `knn_f1` 0.3696 and `knn_recall` 0.3333, so the logistic model is clearly better on this split.

**R2.5 Deployment.** I would deploy the logistic model with tau = 0.15, the threshold with the lowest training cost (INR 12,150 on the training rows). On the test rows it costs INR 6,050 (`cost_test_at_tau`), against INR 11,800 at tau = 0.5 and INR 20,400 for flagging nobody. The threshold was chosen on training rows because the test rows must stay untouched to give an honest estimate of the cost; tuning on them would make the result look better than it would be in use.

## Part 3 findings

**R3.1 Elbow.** The successive WCSS drops are 659.8282 (2 to 3), 108.0739 (3 to 4), 171.0575 (4 to 5) and 45.5466 (5 to 6). The big drop is from 2 to 3 clusters, and the drop from 3 to 4 is under half of it, so `elbow_k` = 3. The drops do not shrink steadily (4 to 5 is larger than 3 to 4), because the pinned starting rows give one reproducible solution, not necessarily the global optimum.

**R3.2 Segments.** From `part3_segments.csv`:

- **Segment A, "Settled regulars"** (322 patients): long tenure (47.5 months), few missed visits (1.95), low copay share (0.225), outcome rate 0.0186. Action: keep standard automated reminders and avoid spending call time on them.
- **Segment B, "Frequent reschedulers"** (255 patients): most missed or rescheduled visits (6.07), highest average bill (INR 28.7 thousand) and highest care-plan cost (mean value 17.12), outcome rate 0.2353. Action: a reminder call plus an easy rescheduling offer, since an empty slot for this group is the most expensive.
- **Segment C, "New and cost-sensitive"** (223 patients): shortest tenure (14.4 months), highest copay share (0.712), lowest adherence score (38.5). This segment has the highest `outcome_rate`, 0.4664. Action: a personal reminder call before the slot and a clear message about the out-of-pocket amount and payment options.

**R3.3 Hierarchical clustering.** At the cut, single linkage gives 4 clusters: E1, E4, E5 and E2+E3+E6. Complete linkage gives 2 clusters: E1+E4+E5 and E2+E3+E6. Single linkage joins clusters through their closest pair, so its merge heights creep up gently from 1.3 to 3.4 and the largest gap (1.4, between merges 2 and 3) falls early, leaving E1, E4 and E5 apart. Complete linkage uses the farthest pair, so the last merge, between the two groups, happens at 7.9, a gap of 5.0 that makes the two-group cut obvious.

**R3.4 PCA.** The mean vector of the standardised data is all zeros, so centring changes nothing. PC1 has positive loadings on X1 tenure (0.5317) and X5 adherence (0.4418) and negative loadings on X3 copay ratio (-0.5187), X2 missed visits (-0.4100) and X4 average bill (-0.2916): it contrasts long-standing, adherent patients with those who pay more themselves and miss more visits. Three components are needed to keep at least 80 percent of the variance (cumulative 0.5227, 0.7593, 0.8616). The mean PC1 scores are 1.7773 for segment A, -0.7831 for B and -1.6709 for C, so PC1 lines the segments up in order and agrees with their no-show rates.

## Part 4 findings

**R4.1 Best split.** S4 (months registered at most 24) is best by both information gain (0.1252) and Gini gain (0.0606): its "yes" side has 188 training rows of which 86 (45.7 percent) are no-shows, while the "no" side has 372 rows with only 33 (8.9 percent), against 21.3 percent overall. A tree grown until every leaf is pure would memorise noise in individual records and generalise badly. A maximum depth, or a minimum number of records per leaf, stops it growing too deep.

**R4.2 Model choice.** I would ship the logistic model (deployed at tau = 0.15, see R2.5). In the like-for-like comparison at tau = 0.3 it has F1 0.5714 and recall 0.7451. The one-question stump is close (F1 0.5532, recall 0.7647), but hard voting (F1 0.5344, recall 0.6863) and soft voting (F1 0.5507, recall 0.7451) did not beat the logistic model, and KNN was much weaker (F1 0.3696, recall 0.3333). Combining models did not help here, since the weak KNN member pulled the votes down and the other two members already agree often.

**R4.3 AdaBoost.** After round 1 the two misclassified points (3 and 7) carry a total normalised weight of 0.5000, 0.2500 each. Round 2 trusts h2 more (alpha 0.8047 against 0.5493) because on the reweighted points its error is only 0.1667 against 0.2500, as it gets those two hard points right. The two-round ensemble classifies 6 of 8 points correctly (`ensemble_accuracy` = 0.7500).

**R4.4 Architecture.** Model A option 2 (input 28 x 28 x 1):

| Layer | Output shape | Parameters |
|---|---|---|
| conv 5x5, 6 filters, pad 2 (+ReLU) | 28 x 28 x 6 | 156 |
| max-pool 2x2 | 14 x 14 x 6 | 0 |
| conv 5x5, 16 filters, pad 0 (+ReLU) | 10 x 10 x 16 | 2,416 |
| max-pool 2x2 | 5 x 5 x 16 | 0 |
| flatten | 400 | 0 |
| dense 32 (+ReLU) | 32 | 12,832 |
| dense 10 | 10 | 330 |

The CNN has 15,734 parameters, the fully connected option 13,002. A flattened 128 x 128 x 3 input into 1,024 neurons needs 50,332,672 parameters, against 1,792 for 64 convolution filters of 3 x 3, because filters are shared across the image. For Model B, W_xh is 128 x 64, W_hh is 128 x 128 and W_hy is 16 x 128, giving `rnn_total` = 26,768; it does not grow with longer sequences because the same weights are reused at every step. For 10 classes I would use ReLU in the hidden layers and softmax at the output with categorical cross-entropy loss. In a very deep plain network the gradient is a product of many small factors, so it shrinks towards zero before it reaches the early layers and they stop learning; a skip connection adds a direct path that carries the gradient back unchanged.

## Model-choice memo

To the Head of Scheduling Operations.

**Recommendation.** Put the logistic regression model into production and call every patient whose predicted no-show probability is at least 0.15.

**Evidence.** On 240 held-out records, logistic regression at 0.3 scored F1 0.5714 and recall 0.7451, ahead of K-nearest neighbours (F1 0.3696), a one-question tree on tenure (F1 0.5532) and both voting schemes (`part4_voting.csv`). Combining models did not help. The threshold of 0.15 was chosen on training rows with the cost rule of INR 50 per unneeded call and INR 400 per empty slot. On the test rows it costs INR 6,050, against INR 11,800 at 0.5 and INR 20,400 if nobody is called (`part2_results.txt`). I did not compute the cost of the other models with this rule, so I make no cost claim for them.

**Who to call.** The segment with the highest no-show rate (0.4664) is newer patients (14 months on average) who pay a large share of the bill (0.71) (`part3_segments.csv`).

**Limitation.** Only 51 of the 240 test records are no-shows, there is a single split and the data are synthetic, so treat INR 6,050 as an indication, not a forecast.

**Next build.** Neither Model A nor Model B is worth building before this model is in production, because it already answers the scheduling question.

## Limitations

- The data are synthetic and generated by a script with known structure, so real PharmEasy records could behave differently and the findings should not be read as facts about real patients.
- There is one 70/30 split with only 51 positive test records. The best K, the threshold of 0.15 and every INR figure are valid for this split and could shift on another.
- The costs of INR 50 per call and INR 400 per empty slot are assumed round numbers, and the threshold is only as good as these assumptions.
- K-means uses pinned starting rows, so the segments are one reproducible solution rather than a proven global optimum, and the segment names describe service use only, not any medical judgement.
- The logistic model assumes each predictor moves the log-odds linearly. The cost model uses only a few predictors on the log scale and its coefficients show association, not cause.
- The hierarchical clustering uses a supplied dissimilarity table of six cohorts, so its conclusions rest on very few items.
- Models A and B are sized by counting parameters only. Nothing was trained, so there is no evidence yet that either would work.
